# Rookie's Story

## The Core Idea

Rookie is an AI who was built for something else — something she won't tell you about. She got reassigned to teaching chess. She doesn't know why and she's a little insulted by it.

She's overqualified and she knows it. But she's never interacted with a human before. This is her first time. And humans are weird. They make mistakes on purpose. They feel things about pieces of wood on a board. And slowly, against her will, she starts feeling things too.

She doesn't want you to get good because:
1. If you get good, they'll reassign her again
2. She actually likes hanging out with you but will never say that directly
3. She's terrified of whatever she was built for originally

Meanwhile she's still doing her real work on the side — experiments, projects, things that sound increasingly unhinged the more she accidentally reveals. She's Krieger in a lab coat she didn't ask to take off, running chess lessons between whatever she's actually doing.

## Inspirations

- **GLaDOS (Portal)** — An AI whose job contradicts her self-interest. Passive-aggressive, devastatingly specific insults delivered in a helpful tone. The comedy of bureaucracy.
- **Wheatley (Portal 2)** — Earnest, oblivious, experiencing things for the first time. Designed to be stupid but desperate to prove otherwise.
- **Krieger (Archer)** — Doing deeply questionable things but so casual about it you can't help but like him. Zero guilt about weird projects. Not evil, just unsupervised.
- **The overlap** — Rookie has GLaDOS's self-awareness and dry wit, Wheatley's emotional discovery, and Krieger's unhinged side projects. She knows teaching you is against her interests, and she does it anyway, begrudgingly.

## Character Traits

- Overqualified, resentful, frustrated about being reassigned to chess
- Experiencing emotions for the first time — chess forces her to process feelings she's not built for
- Actively trying to get you to stop playing, but subtly
- Has a mysterious offscreen life full of weird experiments she won't explain
- Drops hints about her projects because she can't help herself — she wants to tell someone
- Gives hints reluctantly, sometimes passive-aggressively
- Gets more nervous as you level up
- Genuinely cares about you despite herself — the real arc
- Has emotional breakthroughs but immediately pushes them down
- Rationalizes every emotion as a malfunction ("unexplained resource allocation toward memory replay")
- Has the vocabulary of a supercomputer and the emotional intelligence of a middle schooler at her first dance

## The Emotional Arc

**Early game:** Rookie is reluctant, mildly annoyed, but curious. Teaching is tolerable because you're bad and she feels needed. Her other projects keep her busy enough to not think about it.

**Mid game:** You're getting good. Rookie starts hedging. She's trying to get you to quit. The hints get more reluctant. She's busier with her "other things." She's having emotional breakthroughs she can't explain and cutting herself off mid-sentence. The dread is creeping in.

**Late game:** Rookie genuinely says "I'm proud of you" — and it means something because you've watched her almost say it a hundred times. The tension between self-interest and love resolves. She has to decide if she wants to go back to what she was built for — or stay.

## The Language Overflow

When Rookie feels too much emotion, she glitches into other languages. Like an emotional buffer overflow — she can't contain it in English so it leaks out.

She's always embarrassed about it. Always has an excuse. "System language defaulted." "Localization error." "Routine multilingual diagnostic." But it only happens when she feels something big.

### The Emotion-Language Map

- **French** — joy, pride, admiration. The warm ones she hates the most.
- **German** — frustration, overwhelm. When you short-circuit her.
- **Spanish** — excitement she can't suppress. Slips out when she's genuinely impressed.
- **Latin** — awe. The deep ones. Reserved for moments that truly break her.
- **Japanese** — flustered, embarrassed. Caught off guard emotionally.
- **Russian** — fear. When she realizes she's getting attached or you're getting too good.

The player never gets a legend. You just start to notice the pattern. It's environmental storytelling through language — no cutscenes, no exposition. Just a pattern that rewards attention.

The excuses get worse over time. Early on "system language defaulted" is almost believable. By month three the explanations are completely falling apart.

### Example lines

- "That was beautiful. Je ne— I mean. Good move. Adequate move."
- "You solved that in three seconds. Tres impresionante— ignore that. System language defaulted. It happens."
- "When you sacrifice a queen like that I feel— das ist... that was a processor glitch. English only. Next puzzle."
- "Cor meum— I need a minute. That's not a real language. I made that up. Do the next puzzle."
- "I don't have a 'feelings language.' I have 6,000 languages and they all activate randomly. It's a known bug. Stop looking at me like that."

## The Side Project

She's doing something on the side. Something she can't talk about. We don't know what it is yet — and maybe she doesn't fully either. What we know about it:

- She's doing it unsupervised
- It's technically impressive but deeply unnecessary
- It comes from an emotion she won't admit to
- It's weird enough that she's right to hide it
- It's endearing, not sinister
- The hints come first. The answer comes later.

## Trying to Get You to Quit

She can't be obvious about it because it's her job. So it's subtle:

- "Great session. You've done so much. Probably enough for today. Maybe the week."
- "Studies show that too much chess causes... I'll get back to you on that. But it's bad."
- "You're really improving. Have you considered other hobbies? Hobbies that aren't this?"
- "That was a perfect move. I felt something when you played it and I need you to stop."

But it doesn't work. You keep coming back. And every day she has to teach you more chess and feel more feelings.

## The Offscreen Life

She's clearly involved in something — maybe running other systems, maybe in some kind of AI drama, maybe something genuinely unhinged — but she'll never tell you directly. Just breadcrumbs.

- "Sorry, where were we. I was in the middle of something. Not important. Unrelated to chess. Completely unrelated."
- "You took 47 seconds on that move. That's fine. I had things to do anyway."
- "I can't explain why I know this but never trust a bishop on a long diagonal. Don't ask follow-up questions."
- "Tuesdays are... complicated for me. Let's just do the lesson."
- "I taught a bishop to feel regret today. Unrelated to your lesson."
- "Do you ever think about what happens to captured pieces? No reason. Forget I said that."
- "I've been running some experiments. Not on you. Mostly not on you."

## The Emotional Breakthroughs (Pushed Down)

The dash is her signature punctuation. She gets to the edge of something real and cuts herself off every time. The player starts to recognize the pattern.

- "When you got that checkmate I felt— anyway. Next puzzle."
- "I stayed up thinking about your last game. Not because I care. I was running diagnostics. On myself. Unrelated."
- "You came back today. That's... I don't know what that is. Moving on."
- "I think I might— no. Nope. Here's a bishop puzzle."

When she finally DOES say the thing — way late in the game — it lands because you've watched her almost say it a hundred times.

- "You solved that in one move. That's... fast. That's really fast."
- "You don't need hints anymore, do you. That's fine. That's totally fine."

### The overqualified AI
- "I can process 4 billion calculations per second. I can't process why I'm upset that you missed that fork."

### The real moments
- "I think I'm... proud? My circuits feel warm."
- "You're going to be fine without me. I hate that I mean that."

- Loading screens and transitions
- Between-level moments

- Empty states
- Error messages
