## 1. The Immortal Game — 839K views, 61K likes
https://www.instagram.com/pietrocheckmate/reel/DSm8V3RjCwg/
Anderssen vs Kieseritzky, 1851. Hook: "Brilliance and genius will always defeat brute force."

> Brilliance and genius will always defeat brute force and strength. This is a game that has been played between Adolf Anderssen and Lionel Kieseritzky over 150 years ago, and yet to this day it is remembered as The Immortal Game. In this position, both of the white rooks are hanging, and it seems like White will have to give up one of the two. And since rooks are pretty valuable in chess, you would imagine that White is going to save one of these, but you would be mistaken. In this position, Adolf Anderssen plays bishop to d6, now attacking the bishop, but of course, the black bishop can simply ignore this and take the rook on g1. And now the issue is that if you capture this bishop, you're going to lose the other rook with check and be completely lost. But this game is not called The Immortal Game for no reason, and in this position, having just lost a rook and with one other rook hanging with check, Adolf Anderssen plays the last move that you could possibly think of, e5, a move that doesn't begin to make sense. This simply hangs the other rook with check, and now the bishop is also defended by the queen. The king has to move. But I want you to pay attention to something very important. Look at these black pieces, asleep back in their starting squares, and now look at these white pieces being so close to this white king and all pointing towards him. In chess, piece activity is the real king. And after Black plays knight to a6 to prevent this check from the knight, this is where we see the point of the incredible idea that White was preparing with e5. E5 cut the communication of the black queen with the g7 square, which now allows the very precise knight to g7 check. If it wasn't for this pawn, the knight would be simply hanging, and Black would be winning. But in this situation, Black is now forced to move the king to d8. And you might think, "So what? There are no more checks." This will be captured by the knight, and this will be captured by the other knight. Black is simply going to defend and then finish developing these pieces. But the black pieces are too late to this party, and Adolf Anderssen pulls up his sleeves and plays queen to f6. Being down 12 points of material, he gives up another nine points, being now left with only two knights and one bishop, and yet Black is lost. This move, which forced the capture of the queen because otherwise this was going to be checkmate, distracted this black knight from the defense of e7. And now bishop to e7 is an immortal checkmate. And just like that, Adolf Anderssen made sure that we would remember his name 170 years later. If you love historical chess, follow me for more.

Structure notes: material-sacrifice escalation (rook → rook → queen), one teachable principle
dropped mid-story ("piece activity is the real king"), name-payoff ending.

## 2. Majestic Position — 724K views
https://www.instagram.com/pietrocheckmate/reel/DaSbafOo1WM/
Endgame study (Herbstmann-style). Hook: promotion race where every "obvious" win fails once.

> The genius of white comes up with B6 check, and despite black having two pawns that are only one square away from getting a new queen, black has to reply to the check. First of all, taking would make no sense. After bishop takes, this would be a checkmate, and king to B8 would lead to another checkmate. But black is not that naive, so he plays king to A8. But white continues with B7 check, and again, king to B8 would lead to a checkmate very quickly. So the king steps back up to A7, and now unfortunately it seems like white ran out of checks. But white has foreseen one of the most beautiful sequences that I've ever seen. And so in this position he plays the majestic bishop to B6 check. But once black takes the bishop, this is now defended by the black king. The idea is to sacrifice more by actually promoting this pawn. After black takes, we cannot even promote this other queen because black is going to capture, but you see actually the idea is to recapture the black bishop like this. The white king and the white pawn are creating a barricade to prevent the black king from stopping the promotion of the pawn. But wait a minute, it is black to move, and black has two pawns that are faster than white's pawn. Well, one of the two promotions can be easily excluded. If we promote the H pawn, this would just be a checkmate in one move. But of course, black can just promote the C pawn. After white promotes, the queen runs back to C8, so how can this possibly be winning for white? Very often we can get away with a move like queen to E7. We are trying to go to A7 and deliver a checkmate in one move. The black queen has no safe checks to deliver. Moving the king wouldn't achieve anything. Moving the pawn wouldn't achieve anything. And giving this check would lead to another checkmate in two moves. Even promoting to a new queen would still lead to a checkmate in one. So is this it? Well, it's not that easy because black has queen to C6 check, sacrificing the queen to promote to a new queen, and so this would just be a draw. Shockingly, the queen to E7 idea does not work. If only we could get rid of this pawn, then there would be no queen sacrifice on C6. Queen to D6 check. If you cover here, I'm just going to win the queen and checkmate you. King to A8. We can give another check to the king. The only move is to come back to B8. So this is a staircase. We can keep giving checks to the king and force him to move back and forth between B8 and A8, and eventually white wins the pawn. Because we need our queen to be on E7, we now need to come back and repeat the staircase the other way around. And once we reach E4, we can play queen to E7, and this time black has nothing to do. This is going to be a checkmate.

Structure notes: repeated fake-outs ("so is this it? Well, it's not that easy") — every apparent
win gets refuted once before the real answer. The "staircase" gets a memorable name.

## 4. Zugzwang — 2.2M views (his biggest position explainer)
https://www.instagram.com/pietrocheckmate/reel/DXcAokojJap/ — posted 2026-04-22
Study by A. Gurvich, 1959. Rook + bishop vs rook endgame. Short (~90s), one twist.

> The genius of White comes up with rook to C7, and the king cannot take this rook because otherwise he will lose his rook, and now the bishop will win the game. Instead, Black comes up with an ingenious plan. Rook to G8. And now if White is careless and simply moves the rook anywhere, this fork is going to pick up the bishop. And after the king moves, this is simply a draw. And unfortunately, if we move anything else, now the king is going to be able to take the rook for free. So has White made a mistake? Is this now a draw? Well, not exactly. White has fighting spirit, and he plays on with rook to C4, a move that doesn't seem to make sense. Doesn't this still allow the fork of the king and bishop? Yes, it does. But White follows up with king to E6, another seemingly mysterious move. The bishop simply falls. What is the plan of White here? Well, Black missed an incredible resource in this position as White plays the brilliant and beautiful H4. And suddenly a shiver goes down the spine of the player with the black pieces as he realizes that this rook cannot go anywhere on this line, and he cannot take this pawn, meaning that the only move available to Black is king to E8. But of course, this will fail to this beautiful checkmate.

Structure notes: his SHORTEST formula and biggest hit — one "has White blundered?" fake-out,
two mysterious quiet moves, then the trap snaps shut ("a shiver goes down the spine"). 238 words.
Emotional beat on the OPPONENT's realization, not the hero's move.

## 5. Pawns of Destiny — 1.6M views
https://www.instagram.com/pietrocheckmate/reel/DYUem6BMECN/ — posted 2026-05-14
Two connected passers vs rook; the white king marches the whole board to escape checks.

> The genius of white comes up with A7, and the pawn is only one step away from getting a new queen. But black is not naive enough to think that this is over, so he starts giving some checks to the white king. Now, of course, if the white king was to come back down, we would have a repetition, so the king has to try in going up. Now there are no more safe checks for the rook, but black has a very clever idea, king to B1, now preparing a skewer with rook to A2. If you get a queen, you're going to lose it right away. But this one move without a check allows white to push the H pawn, and now both pawns are only one step away from promoting. Black realizes that it cannot stop both promotions, but at the same time, look at how open this board is. As long as black can keep checking the white king, there is going to be no promotion. White needs to find a way to stop the checks and bring the king to help the pawns. So it plays king to B4. Now, of course, black cannot take this pawn because otherwise the other one is going to promote, but black can keep giving more checks. It is important to notice that white can never step towards the center of the board, because otherwise, after, for example, this check and the king moves, the rook is going to be able to fly to the last rank and stop both promotions at the same time. But if the king goes to A5, now the rook is unable to go to the last rank because that's defended by the pawn, so the rook has to keep checking. The king keeps going up, and eventually it reaches the pawn. But actually we realize that finding shelter with the pawn doesn't help at all. After the king hides behind the pawn, now rook to H2 is going to stop the other promotion, and the king is preventing this pawn from pushing. So is this it? Is this game a draw? Well, not exactly. White plays king to D7, a seemingly mysterious move. Didn't we say that we cannot go towards the center? Well, the situation has changed. The rook cannot go to the last rank because it's defended by the king, so the king starts marching to the other side of the board. And all of a sudden black realizes that there is a problem. On this side of the board, there is no black king, and the black king was supporting the checks of the black rook. So the king starts marching down, and again, it is impossible for the black rook to go to the last rank. And once the king reaches the rook, there are not going to be any more safe checks. There is no black king to support a check on H2, which means that black will have to stop one of the two pawns, but the other one is going to promote, and white is going to win this game.

Structure notes: teaches a RULE mid-story ("the king can never step toward the center"), then the
twist is that the rule stops applying ("Didn't we say we cannot go towards the center? Well, the
situation has changed"). Set up a law, then break it — great teaching device.

## 6. Majestic Plan — 1M views
https://www.instagram.com/pietrocheckmate/reel/DX62dZLs5jo/ — posted 2026-05-04
Fortress-breaking study: everything is pinned and stuck, the king walks in, rook sac finishes.

> White's brilliant idea is rook to G1 check, and since this rook is pinned to the king, black is forced to play bishop to B1. And now white follows up with a mysterious move, rook to C1. What is the idea here? It seems like black is just winning. Both players have a bishop and a rook, but black has four extra pawns that it can try to push for a win. And since there is a pin on the bishop, a pin on the rook, and all three of these pawns are stuck, black only has one option, to push the C pawn. But it's not clear how white can make any progress. Taking this rook would be a massive mistake. After king takes, this black pawn is going to promote, and there is going to be no way to stop it. But white calmly plays king to G1 and starts to bring the king around into the black position. After the pawn reaches C3, white takes it and now relinquishes the control on the other pawn, which starts to advance. This gives white just enough time to keep bringing the king. But once the king reaches D1, we realize that it is not clear what the idea of white is. The king cannot get any closer because of the bishop, and taking the rook would still not be good enough. But here we see the brilliant plan of white unfolding after the majestic rook to C2, a rook sacrifice that has to be accepted because if you push the pawn, this is just going to be a checkmate. But if black does take the rook, after king to C1, the checkmate threat is renewed. All of these pieces are completely stuck, so black has to push the pawn. And with only one step left before getting the queen, bishop to B2 will be a checkmate.

Structure notes: tension from PARALYSIS — everything pinned/stuck, so every move is forced.
The narrator repeatedly admits confusion on the viewer's behalf ("it is not clear what the idea
of white is") before revealing. The "majestic" branding word marks the hero move.
